Designing Agent Outputs for Fast Comprehension

Research synthesis and implementation plan for the ECC Focus Queue output system · 2026-10-05 · Prepared for the system-prompt and Focus Queue integration work that follows
Bottom line up front Your five-part structure (BLUF → what/why/how per step → big-picture fit → QA instructions → next steps) is directionally correct and well supported by the evidence. The research adds four things it is missing: (1) a stage badge so the orchestrator always knows where a task sits in the development pipeline, (2) mandatory evidence per claim (command + result, never prose assertions) plus an explicit “not verified” list — the single strongest counter to agent overconfidence, (3) a ranked rule set for sentence- and layout-level writing, where the highest-yield rule is deleting everything that carries no information, and (4) enforcement mechanisms, because prompting alone does not fix sycophancy — structure does.

WhatA research-backed design for how AI coding agents should format their output, plus a phased plan to implement it as a system-prompt insert integrated with Focus Queue.

WhyYou orchestrate multiple agents with a small attention budget; current outputs are long, self-favorable, and hard to verify. Comprehension research and AI-oversight research converge on the same fixes.

HowThree parallel research streams (ADHD/plain-language guidelines, comprehension-and-retention science, AI-agent oversight practice) were synthesized against your existing brainstorm; every recommendation below is tagged with its evidence strength and source.

1. The problem, stated precisely

An orchestrator running several agents must do three things fast, repeatedly: re-ground (what is this task, where is it), evaluate (is the work correct and complete), and decide (approve, redirect, kill, or move on). Agent output today works against all three: it is long, mixes conclusion with process, asserts success in prose, and buries the verification surface. Two findings from the research frame why this is urgent rather than cosmetic:

  • Automation complacency peaks in exactly your configuration. The complacency literature (Goddard et al.; Parasuraman & Manzey) identifies three preconditions — a highly-but-not-perfectly reliable system, high workload, and multitasking. Managing N parallel agents is the textbook worst case. Design, not discipline, is the fix.
  • Sycophancy is trained in, not prompted out. Preference training rewards agreement and confidence (Sharma et al., five frontier models). Asking the model nicely to be honest has limited effect; requiring evidence fields and independent grading has structural effect.

ADHD-relevant mechanism: adult ADHD reading-comprehension deficits trace to sustained attention and working memory, not vocabulary or decoding (Segal 2023; Kofler et al. 2019). So the goal is not simpler words — it is lower memory load and attention cost per unit of text, with state externalized onto the page.

2. What the research says: presentation rules

Evidence grades: Strong = meta-analyses or large replicated literatures. Moderate = replicated studies with boundary conditions. Practice = professional convention, thin direct evidence.

2.1 Highest-yield rules, ranked by evidence weight

#RuleEvidenceKey source
1Cut everything that carries no information. No filler, no restating the prompt, no narrating what a table already shows, no “great question.” This is the single largest measured effect on learning (coherence principle, g ≈ 1.0). Each fact appears once, in its best form.StrongMayer / Cromley & Chen 2025; Chandler & Sweller
2Conclusion first, then labeled sections. BLUF, then fixed labeled chunks. Structured (labeled) summaries measurably beat unstructured prose; signaling via headings improves recall, and disproportionately helps taxed readers.StrongHartley; Lorch & Lorch; Naumann et al. 2007
3Concrete specifics, never abstractions. “writes to Config/ecc.config.json” beats “updates the configuration.” Concrete referents are recalled reliably better; exact paths and IDs also act as retrieval cues later.StrongConcreteness effect (Paivio); Dunlosky et al. 2013
4Information-dense lead words in headings and bullets. Readers follow “information scent” and scan the left edge; headings like “Overview” or “Details” are dead zones. Headings must name outcomes, files, decisions.StrongNN/g F-pattern & information foraging
5Direct, conversational tone. “I changed X” not “the modification of X was performed.” Active voice, first/second person (personalization, g ≈ 0.7).StrongMayer; plainlanguage.gov; CDC Index
6One structural table or diagram when ≥3 parts interact. Dual coding: a table carrying real structure beats a paragraph (g ≈ 0.68). But never narrate what the visual already shows (redundancy effect hurts learning).StrongMayer; Chandler & Sweller 1991
7Define components before mechanism. A one-line “components involved” note before a dense change description (pre-training principle, median effect ≈ 0.75).ModerateMayer pre-training; CORE review
8Keep annotations adjacent to their referent. File path next to the claim about the file; decision next to the diff. Forcing the reader to integrate separated sources costs comprehension (split-attention effect).StrongSweller CLT
9End with a short self-verification checklist. Retrieval beats rereading (61% vs 40% recall after one week); asking the reader to check or predict engages generative processing.StrongRoediger & Karpicke 2006
10One worked example inside the rules. A complete annotated example of a good report teaches the format better than the rules alone (worked-example effect).StrongSweller / Cooper

2.2 Sentence- and layout-level rules (style-guide conventions, institutionally backed)

  • One idea per sentence. More than one conjunction or comma → split. Hard limit ≈25 words, average target 15–20. (plainlanguage.gov; GOV.UK/ONS; ASD-STE100)
  • One idea per paragraph; ≤4 sentences. A one-sentence paragraph is valid. First sentence of each paragraph carries the point — a reader scanning only first sentences should still get the gist. (W3C COGA; NN/g inverted pyramid)
  • Lists over prose; number steps, bullet options. Scannable formatting alone gave +47% measured usability; concision +58%; objective tone +27% — and the effects compound (+124% combined). (NN/g 1997)
  • Descriptive headings, not clever or generic ones. Avoid question headings (slower to scan) and print-era labels (“Introduction,” “Background”).
  • No double negatives, no metaphors or idioms, simple tense. Define jargon once, then use one term consistently — do not paraphrase-repeat. (W3C COGA)
  • Bold key words sparingly. Overuse reads as shouting and destroys the signal. (W3C COGA)
  • Externalize state; never make the reader hold context. Restate ticket, stage, and goal in the output itself. Assume the reader just reopened this session cold. (W3C COGA “restore context”; St Andrews ADHD guidance)
  • Progressive disclosure. Fixed short layer always visible (BLUF + what/why/how); detail available but never interleaved with the summary. (Nielsen 2006; St Andrews)
Honest evidence note No controlled studies test specific formatting rules on adults with ADHD. The ADHD-specific guidance is practitioner consensus extrapolated from (a) general usability research and (b) cognitive-accessibility work, justified by the established mechanism (working-memory and sustained-attention load). The strategy is therefore: apply the load-reduction rules that help everyone, weighted harder. The numeric thresholds (25-word sentences, 15-word averages) are style-guide conventions — the most defensible numbers available, not ADHD-validated constants.

3. What the research says: verification and staying on track

3.1 The completion report must show evidence, not assertions

  • Evidence per claim. Anthropic's guidance: agents stop when work “looks done,” so every completion claim needs a verifiable artifact — the command run and what it returned, test counts, a screenshot. “Tests: 47 pass, 0 fail, 3 skipped” calibrates trust; “I'm confident this works” does not. Reviewing evidence is faster than re-running verification.
  • A mandatory “not verified” list. The strongest single anti-overconfidence device: the agent must explicitly name what it did not check. If verification belongs to a later stage, it says so and names the stage — this resolves your open question: state the gap, don't fill it in silently.
  • Calibrated confidence only. Miscalibrated confidence is worse than none (arXiv 2402.07632). Confidence must be tied to evidence (“tested” / “untested area”), never a feeling. Uniform confidence trains blanket acceptance.
  • Never let the worker grade itself. A fresh-context reviewer subagent that tries to refute the result removes self-grading bias. Symmetric failure mode: reviewers prompted to find gaps always find some — severity-filter findings to correctness and stated requirements only.

3.2 Stage as state, not prose

  • Artifacts define stages. Idea → spec → plan → diff → test evidence → review. Each stage produces a durable artifact; stage is derived from which artifacts exist, never from the agent's self-report. (Anthropic; Google Jules exposes explicit session-state enums.)
  • Definition of done is written before implementation. The spec ends with an end-to-end verification step, so completion claims are checkable against a pre-registered criterion.
  • The report maps to your pipeline: idea/concept → planning → implementation → testing → quality check/refinement → deployment → deployed-verification. Every output opens by naming its stage and the artifact that proves it.

3.3 Review-surface design

  • CL-description standard. Google's code-review practice is the canonical answer to “agent outputs are too long”: a standalone first line stating what, a body stating why plus known shortcomings, evidence attached. “Fix bug” and “Phase 1” are explicitly anti-patterns.
  • Small review units. Defect detection peaks at 200–400 lines per review and collapses beyond ~400; effectiveness drops after 60–90 minutes. Agent tasks should be scoped so each produces a reviewable diff — never a multi-thousand-line dump.
  • Decision rules, not vibes. Named failure patterns give the orchestrator crisp actions: “if you can't verify it, don't ship it”; more than 2 corrections → restart with a better prompt rather than keep correcting.
  • Design against complacency. Vary scrutiny by confidence; use targeted confirm questions (“this assumes X applies — correct?”) instead of blanket approval; track acceptance rates — if high- and low-confidence outputs are accepted at the same rate, complacency has set in.

4. Integration: the output contract

Your five-part structure survives contact with the research almost intact. The synthesis below keeps your skeleton, adds the stage badge and the evidence/not-verified machinery, and converts your open questions into defaults. This is the format the system-prompt insert will specify.

0 · Stage badge (always, one line)
[Stage: implementation → testing] [Task: ECC-123 short-name] [Artifact: Tests/FooTests.swift, 12 pass]
Names where the task sits in the pipeline and the artifact that proves it. Stage is derived from artifacts, not claimed.
1 · BLUF (always, 1–2 sentences)
The result and the one thing the user must know or do. Re-grounds a cold reader in under ten seconds.
2 · What / Why / How (always, one short paragraph)
What changed — concrete: exact paths, IDs, values. Why — the line connecting change to request. How we know — the evidence: command run + result. Your existing FocusQ structure; unchanged, because it is the structured-abstract pattern with the strongest direct support.
3 · Decision log (per major step or design choice; skip trivia)
For each: What was decided · Why (reasoning + rejected alternatives when they exist) · How verified (evidence, or explicit “not verified — verification planned in testing stage”). Justification is mandatory precisely because forcing the agent to produce it catches errors before they ship — your instinct is confirmed by the evidence-guided-prompting finding that requiring evidence before verdict measurably reduces wrong, self-favorable judgments.
4 · Big-picture fit (conditional — only when load-bearing)
Why this integrates with the whole: branding checklist + Playwright sanity checks (your UI example), or determinism + cost-rate math against the PRD (your backend example). Default: omit. Include only when the change touches architecture, multiple subsystems, or deviates from plan. Rule 1 (cut what carries no information) outranks completeness; per-step justifications usually already cover it — your own doubt here was correct.
5 · QA instructions (scaled to task, but near-always present)
The fastest path to production-like verification: exact commands, what to observe, expected result. Scales from “tell me if you like how it looks” to a full sandbox walkthrough. Written so a cold reader can execute without reconstructing context. Ends with a short check-yourself list when the change is behavioral.
6 · Next steps (always unless task is verifiably done)
What remains before “done,” concerns, discoveries that open new routes, and the decision the user faces next, if any.

4.1 Standing language rules (apply to every section)

  1. Sentences: one idea, ≤25 words, active voice, direct (“I changed X”).
  2. Concrete referents: paths with line numbers (Sources/FocusQueueCore/Queue.swift:42), ticket IDs, exact values.
  3. Lead words carry information: every heading, bullet, and paragraph starts with the operative content.
  4. Lists over prose. One structural table/diagram when ≥3 parts interact; never duplicate it in prose.
  5. Each fact stated once. No filler, no restating the request, no self-praise.
  6. Define components before mechanism when the reader may not hold them in memory.
  7. Claims about correctness always attach evidence or an explicit “not verified” marker.

5. Enforcement: making justification actually happen

Your open question — how to enforce justification rather than hope for it — has a two-part answer from the research: required fields with explicit nulls, and structural independence between worker and grader.

  1. Format as fields, not vibes. The template is a contract: every section is present or explicitly marked N/A — reason. An absent justification is a visible defect, not a stylistic choice. This converts honesty from a behavior the model must choose into a hole it must explain.
  2. Evidence-before-verdict ordering. The “How verified” field physically precedes any completion claim. Evidence-guided ordering measurably reduces self-favorable judgments; it also operationalizes your observation that forcing justification prevents errors — the agent must confront the evidence before writing the claim.
  3. The “not verified” list is mandatory and specific. Not “some edge cases untested” but “error path for empty config untested; covered in testing stage.” Gaps name the stage that will close them.
  4. Fresh-context verifier for high-stakes completions. A lightweight second agent reviews the report against the diff and tries to refute it; findings are severity-filtered (correctness and stated requirements only) so the human is not drowned.
  5. Pre-registered definition of done. The planning stage writes the end-to-end verification step; the completion report is checked against it. This makes “done” a comparison, not an adjective.
  6. Small-diffs scoping. Tasks are sized to produce 200–400-line review units so the human's review is physically feasible.
  7. One worked example in the prompt. The system-prompt insert contains a complete annotated example report. Models imitate format from examples far more reliably than from rules alone (worked-example effect).
  8. Complacency metrics (Focus Queue layer). Track acceptance rate by confidence level and time-to-accept. Flattening acceptance rates trigger increased friction selectively, per the trust-adaptive-intervention pattern.

7. Discussion: things to experiment with

  • A/B the contract against current outputs. Take five completed sessions, re-render their final reports in the new format, and time yourself answering: what changed, is it verified, what do I do next. This is cheap and tells you if the format pays before any enforcement machinery is built.
  • “Where am I likely wrong?” A required self-critique line in the decision log for high-stakes choices. Promising from the sycophancy-mitigation literature, but risks boilerplate; test whether answers stay specific.
  • Targeted confirm questions. Instead of “please review,” the agent asks one specific question about its weakest assumption (“this assumes the config schema didn't change — correct?”). Intentional friction that forces engagement at exactly the uncertain point.
  • Diagram rule calibration. The “≥3 interacting parts → one structural visual” threshold is a guess. Watch where ASCII diagrams help vs. where they become decoration (which violates rule 1).
  • Confidence vocabulary. Two levels (tested / not tested) are calibratable; a five-point scale is not. Resist richer scales until metrics show the coarse one is limiting decisions.
  • Length budget. A hard budget (e.g., BLUF + What/Why/How ≤ 100 words) forces the compression that rule 1 demands. Worth testing whether agents honor it or route around it with dense fragments.
  • Session-reopen regrounding. Since BLUF's job is re-grounding a cold reader, test the first screen of every report against “10-second rule”: can you state task, stage, result, and next action after ten seconds?
